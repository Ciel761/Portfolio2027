Patterns of Hypertext-Augmented Sensemaking

2024

UIST 2024 · Siyi Zhu, Robert Haisfield, Brendan Langen, and Joel Chan

Modern hypertext notebooks promise more than connected documents: they can help scholars resume interrupted thinking, recover ideas distributed across time and tools, and develop source material into new synthesis. Guided tours with 23 scholars revealed two families of practices: explicit note types that advance stages of sensemaking, and associative structures - hubs, indices, and incubators - that make fragmented information usable again.

The Fragmentation Problem

Temporal fragmentation
Long projects are repeatedly interrupted. When scholars return, their context, questions, and interpretation may have changed.

Spatial fragmentation
Papers, annotations, provisional notes, and developed ideas become distributed across pages, folders, tools, and devices.

The difficult work is often the “meta-work” of getting back into position to think: locating prior material, restoring context, and deciding what should happen next.

Guided Tours of Scholarly Work

Participants
The core sample included 16 scholars using newer hypertext notebooks. Seven scholars using mainstream tools such as Google Docs provided comparison cases.

Procedure
Remote guided tours lasted approximately 1 to 1.5 hours. Participants shared their screens and walked through real sensemaking workflows, structures, artifacts, changes, and pain points.

Analysis
Full transcripts and screen recordings were analyzed thematically. Open coding and constant comparison developed lower-level actions such as linking and querying into patterns including hubs, incubators, and temporo-spatial fragmentation.

Explicit Note Types

Source notes
Represent papers and other source material as referenceable, linkable objects with provenance and metadata.

Summary notes
Compress a result, quotation, or idea from one source into a reusable unit while preserving a link to its origin.

Synthesis notes
Combine summaries into higher-level concepts, questions, findings, and hypotheses that can guide new reading, experiments, and writing.

Hubs and Indices

Hubs
Often-empty pages use contextual backlinks as waypoints to related notes distributed throughout a notebook.

Indices
Curated links and structure searches gather notes into more deliberate topical views that can update as the notebook changes.

Incubators

Incubators develop ideas over time. They combine links, dated records, questions, tasks, and provenance, sometimes adding structure searches that retrieve relevant material automatically.

Design Implications

The observed patterns extend classic hypertext ideas through granular contextual backlinks, flexible many-to-many organization, formal note schemas, and dynamic structure searches. Together they show how hypertext can support the evolving state of thought rather than merely connect finished documents.

Match the work
Let people define information types that correspond to their own stages of sensemaking and provide actions that help each type move forward.

Support associative retrieval
Let people construct dynamic hubs, indices, and incubators without forcing their knowledge into one fixed hierarchy.

Lower the cost of structure
End-user programming and mixed-initiative AI could make powerful structures easier to create while preserving user control and meaning.
